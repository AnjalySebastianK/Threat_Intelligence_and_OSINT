# Threat Intelligence Fundamentals

## What is Threat Intelligence
- Threat Intelligence refers to the process of collecting, analyzing, and applying information about potential or current cyber threats.
- It is not just raw data but contextualized knowledge that helps organizations understand who their adversaries are, what methods they use, and why they are targeting specific systems.
- The purpose of threat intelligence is to enable proactive defense by anticipating attacks, reducing risks, and improving incident response.
- It transforms technical indicators into actionable insights that security teams can use to strengthen their defenses.

## Intelligence Lifecycle
The intelligence lifecycle is a structured process that ensures threat intelligence is collected, processed, and applied effectively. It consists of several stages:
1. **Planning** – Defining the requirements and objectives of intelligence, such as identifying which threats are most relevant to the organization.
2. **Collection** – Gathering data from multiple sources including logs, open-source intelligence, dark web monitoring, and threat feeds.
3. **Processing** – Filtering, normalizing, and organizing raw data so that it can be analyzed meaningfully.
4. **Analysis** – Interpreting the processed data to identify patterns, attacker tactics, and potential risks.
5. **Dissemination** – Sharing the intelligence with stakeholders such as SOC teams, executives, or other departments.
6. **Feedback** – Reviewing the effectiveness of the intelligence and refining future requirements.

## Strategic Intelligence
- Strategic intelligence is the highest-level form of threat intelligence.
- It is designed for executives, board members, and senior decision-makers who need to understand the broader landscape of cyber threats.
- This type of intelligence does not focus on individual incidents but instead examines long-term patterns, geopolitical influences, and industry-wide risks.
- For example, strategic intelligence may analyze how nation-state actors are shifting their focus from financial institutions to energy companies, or how global regulations such as GDPR influence attacker behavior.
- The purpose is to help organizations make informed decisions about investments, policies, and long-term security strategies. Strategic intelligence often comes in the form of white papers, annual threat reports, or executive briefings that highlight trends and future risks.
- It enables leadership to align cybersecurity with business objectives, allocate resources effectively, and prepare for emerging threats before they materialize.

## Tactical Intelligence
- Tactical intelligence is focused on the methods and techniques that adversaries use to carry out attacks.
- It is highly relevant for SOC analysts, incident responders, and technical defenders who need to understand how attackers operate in practice.
- This type of intelligence provides details about attacker tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs), often mapped against frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK.
- For example, tactical intelligence may describe how attackers are using spear-phishing emails with malicious attachments, or how they exploit vulnerabilities in widely used software.
- The goal is to enable defenders to build detection rules, improve monitoring, and strengthen preventive controls.
- Tactical intelligence is usually delivered through technical reports, detection signatures, or playbooks that explain how to recognize and counter specific attack techniques.
- It bridges the gap between strategic planning and operational defense, ensuring that security teams know exactly what to look for and how to respond.

## Operational Intelligence
- Operational intelligence is the most immediate and actionable form of threat intelligence.
- It provides real-time or near real-time insights into ongoing campaigns, active adversaries, and current attack infrastructure.
- This type of intelligence is critical during incident response because it gives defenders the context they need to act quickly.
- For example, operational intelligence may reveal that a ransomware group is actively scanning for unpatched VPN devices, or that a phishing campaign is targeting employees in a specific region.
- It includes details such as attacker IP addresses, domain names, malware hashes, and command-and-control servers. Operational intelligence is often shared through alerts, threat bulletins, or direct feeds into security tools such as SIEMs and EDR platforms.
- The purpose is to enable immediate defensive measures such as blocking malicious traffic, isolating compromised systems, or deploying emergency patches.
- Operational intelligence ensures that organizations can respond to threats as they unfold, reducing the impact of attacks and preventing further damage.

## Summary
- Threat intelligence is a structured discipline that transforms raw data into actionable knowledge.
- The intelligence lifecycle ensures that information is collected, processed, analyzed, and shared in a way that continuously improves security posture.
- Strategic intelligence helps leadership make long-term decisions, tactical intelligence equips defenders with knowledge of adversary techniques, and operational intelligence provides immediate insights during active incidents.
- Together, these three layers of intelligence create a comprehensive defense strategy that allows organizations to anticipate, detect, and respond to threats effectively.
- By understanding and applying each type of intelligence, organizations can strengthen their resilience against evolving cyber adversaries.
